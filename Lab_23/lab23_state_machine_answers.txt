Lab 23 State-Machine Answers
============================

Question 1: Should we change our state to EATING?

Yes, the philosopher must eventually enter the EATING state before leaving
take_forks(). The important detail is that take_forks() does not directly force
that change after the while-loop. Instead, the change happens only through the
test(i) function after the program verifies that the philosopher is HUNGRY and
that neither adjacent neighbor is currently EATING.

Question 2: Who is changing our state to EATING?

There are two valid cases:

1. The philosopher can change its own state indirectly by calling test(i)
   inside take_forks(). If both neighbors are not EATING, test(i) changes
   state[i] from HUNGRY to EATING and signals cond[i].

2. A neighbor can change the philosopher's state indirectly when that neighbor
   finishes eating and calls put_forks(). Inside put_forks(), the neighbor calls
   test(left(i)) and test(right(i)). If the waiting philosopher is now HUNGRY
   and both adjacent neighbors are not EATING, test() changes that waiting
   philosopher's state to EATING and signals the corresponding condition
   variable.

Short explanation of the control flow:

- take_forks() sets state[i] to HUNGRY.
- test(i) decides whether the philosopher may advance to EATING.
- If test(i) cannot promote the philosopher, pthread_cond_wait() blocks the
  thread.
- When a neighbor finishes and executes put_forks(), that neighbor re-runs
  test() on adjacent philosophers.
- The blocked philosopher wakes up only after some execution of test() has
  already changed state[i] to EATING.
